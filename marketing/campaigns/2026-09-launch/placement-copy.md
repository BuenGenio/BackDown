Draft · Asset: carousel/Before-you-clear-that-drive.pdf

A folder name is a label. A content hash gives you evidence about the file itself.

BackDown uses SHA-256 hashing when checking matching file contents. The public alpha includes index, hash, dupes and check commands, so you can move from a list of files to a more informed view of where the matching copies are.

That distinction matters when an archive contains renamed files, repeated exports and years of copied folders. Familiar names can hide different contents; different names can point to matching data.

The result still needs to be read in the context of the drives and files you have scanned. It is evidence to inspect when planning your archive.

## 2026-10-22 09:30 BST — linkedin — Names do not prove content

Draft · Asset: graphics/P11.png

Consider two files both named “holiday.jpg”. They could be different photos.

Now consider “holiday.jpg” and “holiday-copy.jpg”. They could contain exactly the same photo data.

Names help people organise things, but they do not establish that file contents match. That becomes a problem when years of exports, renamed folders and device backups meet in one archive.

BackDown's alpha uses content hashing as part of its duplicate checks. It helps you investigate copies by their data, while keeping the paths available so you can understand where the results came from.

A match is still something to interpret within your backup plan. Start with evidence, then decide which copies serve a purpose.
